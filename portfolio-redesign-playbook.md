# Ruben Costa — Developer Portfolio Redesign Playbook

> **Purpose:** Redesign the portfolio as an editorial, story-driven experience that explains how Ruben became the developer he is today, demonstrates his technical ability through real projects, and makes it easy for recruiters or potential collaborators to evaluate his work.
>
> **Core concept:** *The Developer's Journey* — a personal journal, engineering notebook, and professional portfolio combined.

---

## 1. The goal

The portfolio should communicate three things within the first few minutes:

1. **Who I am:** my background, interests, personality, and professional direction.
2. **How I got here:** the experiences, experiments, challenges, and decisions that shaped my development.
3. **What I can do today:** evidence of my skills through projects, technical decisions, and outcomes.

The site is not meant to be a conventional online CV. It should feel personal and memorable while remaining professional, easy to navigate, and quick to use.

### Success criteria

- A visitor can understand who I am and what I do within a few seconds.
- A visitor can jump directly to projects without reading the entire story.
- The story is chronological enough to follow, but does not pretend that learning was perfectly linear.
- Every major skill claim is supported by a project, experience, or concrete example.
- Projects explain decisions and outcomes, not just technologies.
- The design is distinctive without distracting from the content.
- The site works well on mobile, is accessible, and loads quickly.

---

## 2. Design direction

### Recommended visual identity

Use a **minimal editorial design** with a warm, human tone and precise technical details.

| Element | Direction |
|---|---|
| Overall feel | Editorial, personal, modern, confident, thoughtful |
| Background | Warm off-white or a soft neutral |
| Main text | Charcoal / dark grey rather than pure black |
| Secondary text | Muted grey with sufficient contrast |
| Accent | Restrained burnt orange or muted orange |
| Typography | Modern sans-serif for body and headings; monospace for dates, labels, code-related details |
| Layout | Strong grid, generous whitespace, varied content widths |
| Images | Real project screenshots, prototypes, work-in-progress images, and selected personal photographs |
| Borders | Subtle and consistent; avoid excessive cards and containers |
| Animation | Small, purposeful transitions that support the narrative |

### Visual principles

- Let typography, spacing, layout, and imagery create the identity.
- Use orange as an accent, not as the dominant background colour.
- Keep long-form text comfortably readable. Avoid very wide paragraphs.
- Use visual hierarchy to distinguish chapter titles, dates, captions, and body copy.
- Keep the same design language across the homepage and project case studies.
- Use real images wherever possible. A real prototype is more convincing than generic stock photography.

### Avoid a generic developer aesthetic

Do not rely on the usual combination of a black background, neon gradients, glowing borders, floating code snippets, and constant terminal decorations. Use technical visual elements only when they have a real connection to the story or project.

## 3. Information architecture and page structure

Recommended homepage order:

1. **Hero — The introduction**
2. **Chapter 01 — The beginning**
3. **Chapter 02 — Experimentation**
4. **Chapter 03 — Growth**
5. **Selected projects — The work**
6. **Skills and engineering approach**
7. **Chapter 04/05 — The present and what comes next**
8. **Contact**

The exact chapter count and titles can change once the real story is written. Do not add chapters just to make the page longer.

### Global navigation

Keep a small, consistent navigation visible or easy to reach:

- Story
- Projects
- Skills
- About / Now
- Contact
- CV / Résumé download

The navigation must let recruiters skip the story and go straight to the work. A sticky navigation is useful if it stays compact and does not consume too much screen space.

### 3.1 Hero — The introduction

**Purpose:** Communicate identity, direction, and the central idea of the portfolio immediately.

Include:

- Name.
- A clear description of what kind of developer I am or what I focus on.
- A short, specific statement that introduces the journey.
- A primary action to explore the story or selected projects.
- A secondary action to view/download the CV or contact me.
- Optional personal image, workspace photograph, or collage of actual work.

Possible direction for the headline:

> Curiosity brought me here. Building things made me a developer.

This is a placeholder, not mandatory final copy. The final statement should sound natural and reflect my real experience.

**Do:** make the first screen clear, memorable, and useful.

**Don't:** use a vague headline such as “Welcome to my portfolio”, make visitors guess what I do, or put a long biography in the hero.

### 3.2 Chapter 01 — The beginning

**Purpose:** Explain where the interest in technology started.

Potential content:

- First contact with computers, electronics, or technology.
- What made me curious.
- Early influences or people who encouraged me.
- The first thing I tried to build or understand.
- A photograph, object, or project that represents this stage.

Keep this section personal, but relevant to the development of my technical interests.

### 3.3 Chapter 02 — Experimentation

**Purpose:** Show the transition from curiosity to hands-on learning.

Potential content:

- Early hardware or electronics experiments.
- IoT or microcontroller work, where relevant.
- First programming exercises or applications.
- Mistakes, failed attempts, and discoveries.
- The first project that made software development feel real.

Use actual images of prototypes, devices, code, notes, or early projects. If no image exists, use a simple typographic treatment rather than inventing visual evidence.

### 3.4 Chapter 03 — Growth

**Purpose:** Explain how technical ability and engineering thinking developed.

Potential content:

- Education, courses, self-directed learning, or work experience.
- Moving from isolated exercises to complete applications.
- Learning frontend, backend, databases, APIs, testing, or deployment.
- Understanding architecture, maintainability, security, and performance.
- One or two important technical challenges and what changed after solving them.

Connect learning to specific experiences. Avoid turning the chapter into a list of technologies.

### 3.5 Selected projects — The work

**Purpose:** Prove what I can build.

Show **3–5 strongest projects** on the homepage. Prioritise depth and relevance over quantity. Link to a complete case study for each major project. Put smaller experiments and older work in a separate archive if needed.

A project should be featured when it demonstrates one or more of the following:

- Strong technical problem-solving.
- Meaningful product thinking.
- A useful or interesting outcome.
- A skill relevant to the roles or opportunities I want.
- Clear personal contribution and ownership.

### 3.6 Skills and engineering approach

Group skills by capability rather than presenting one long list.

Possible groups:

- **Frontend:** languages, frameworks, UI, accessibility, responsive design.
- **Backend:** server-side development, APIs, authentication, validation.
- **Data:** relational databases, SQL, ORM, data modelling.
- **Engineering practices:** Git, testing, debugging, documentation, deployment.
- **Other areas:** hardware, IoT, automation, or other genuine experience.

Only include technologies I can explain and discuss honestly. Indicate whether a technology is used in a real project, studied, or currently being explored when that distinction matters.

### 3.7 The present — Now and next

**Purpose:** Show what I am focused on today and where I want to develop.

Include:

- Current technical interests.
- What I am building or learning now.
- The kind of problems I enjoy solving.
- Professional direction or types of opportunities I am open to.

Keep this section current. Remove outdated goals and old status statements when they stop being true.

### 3.8 Contact

Make the next action obvious.

Include relevant contact methods, professional profiles, GitHub, LinkedIn, and a CV link. Do not make visitors search through the page to find how to contact me.

## 4. Storytelling rules

The story should feel like a real journey, not a marketing narrative manufactured after the fact.

### Use this structure for each milestone

1. **When:** year, period, or life stage. Use an exact year only when known.
2. **What happened:** the experience, project, decision, or challenge.
3. **Why it mattered:** what changed in my interests, understanding, or direction.
4. **What I learned:** the skills or lessons gained.
5. **What came next:** how this milestone led to the next stage.

### Storytelling do's

- Use first-person writing (“I built…”, “I struggled with…”, “I learned…”).
- Be specific: describe an actual problem, decision, or discovery.
- Include setbacks and changes of direction where they genuinely happened.
- Connect technical growth to real experiences.
- Use short paragraphs, clear headings, and captions.
- Keep the tone confident but honest.
- Let the projects provide evidence for the claims in the story.

### Storytelling don'ts

- Do not invent dates, achievements, client results, responsibilities, or motivations.
- Do not claim that every milestone was planned or that learning was perfectly linear.
- Do not use generic statements such as “I have always been passionate about technology” without a concrete example.
- Do not turn every paragraph into a list of technologies.
- Do not exaggerate the difficulty or impact of a project.
- Do not include personal details that do not contribute to the story or that I do not want public.

## 5. Project case-study template

Every featured project should explain the problem, my contribution, the implementation, and the result. Keep the format consistent so visitors can compare projects easily.

Use this template for each project:

```markdown
# [Project name]

**One-line summary:** [What it does and who it is for.]

- **Type:** [Personal / academic / professional / open source]
- **Status:** [Completed / in progress / archived]
- **Role:** [What I personally designed, implemented, or maintained]
- **Timeline:** [Accurate dates or duration, if known]
- **Live demo:** [URL, if available]
- **Source code:** [Repository URL, if public]

## The idea
What problem, need, or curiosity motivated this project?

## The challenge
What was technically or product-wise difficult?

## My approach
What did I decide to build, and why? Explain relevant architecture or design decisions.

## Implementation
Describe the important features and the parts I personally implemented.

## Technology
- [Technology]: [How and why it was used]
- [Technology]: [How and why it was used]

## The result
What works today? Include evidence, screenshots, a demo, or measurable results only when available.

## What I learned
What did this project teach me? What would I improve if I rebuilt it today?
```

### Project presentation do's

- Use high-quality screenshots from the real application.
- Show the product in context, not only a logo or technology badges.
- Explain why important technical decisions were made.
- Clearly distinguish my contribution from team or third-party work.
- Link to a working demo and repository when they are safe and available.
- Mention limitations or unfinished parts when relevant.
- Use diagrams only when they explain architecture or data flow better than text.

### Project presentation don'ts

- Do not display technology logos without explaining how the technology was used.
- Do not claim ownership of code or features built by someone else.
- Do not invent users, performance metrics, revenue, or business impact.
- Do not expose API keys, credentials, private URLs, customer data, or confidential source code.
- Do not present a mockup as a finished, working product.
- Do not make every project card look identical if the project screenshots need different layouts, but keep typography and spacing consistent.

## 6. Skills: show evidence, not percentages

Avoid skill meters such as “JavaScript — 90%” or “PostgreSQL — 80%”. These percentages have no clear measurement standard and are difficult to verify.

Instead, connect each important skill to evidence:

| Skill | Evidence to show |
|---|---|
| JavaScript / TypeScript | A feature, application, or technical problem implemented with it |
| React / Next.js | A real interface, route structure, server/client decision, or application |
| Node.js / APIs | Backend functionality, validation, integration, or API design |
| SQL / PostgreSQL | Data model, query, relationship, or database decision |
| Prisma or another ORM | Schema design, migrations, queries, or data-access implementation |
| Git | Collaboration, branching, commit history, or contribution workflow |
| Testing | Tests written, bugs caught, or quality improvements |

Only use examples that genuinely apply. The table is a guide, not a claim that every skill has already been demonstrated.

### Skill-label guidance

When useful, distinguish between:

- **Used in projects:** applied in a real implementation.
- **Familiar with:** used or studied enough to explain the fundamentals.
- **Currently learning:** an active learning goal, not yet a proven strength.

Avoid presenting self-assessed levels as objective facts.

## 7. Interaction and motion

Interactions should make the site easier or more engaging to explore, not create friction.

### Recommended interactions

- Compact sticky navigation.
- Subtle scroll progress indicator for the story.
- Gentle chapter entrance transitions.
- Clear hover and focus states on project links.
- Optional image enlargement or gallery for project screenshots.
- Small transitions between page sections or case studies.
- A visible active chapter indicator if it improves orientation.

### Motion do's

- Keep animations short and purposeful.
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Ensure content is readable before or without animation.
- Keep navigation and buttons responsive while animations run.
- Test on lower-powered mobile devices.

### Motion don'ts

- Do not animate every element on scroll.
- Do not block reading while waiting for an entrance animation.
- Do not use scroll-jacking or hijack normal browser scrolling.
- Do not make essential information available only on hover.
- Do not add a 3D experience, custom cursor, sound, or complex canvas effect unless it directly supports the content and remains usable without it.

---

## 8. UX, accessibility, and responsive design

### Navigation and usability

- Keep Story, Projects, Skills, and Contact easy to find.
- Make the CV and important external profiles accessible.
- Use descriptive link text (avoid repeated “Click here”).
- Make interactive elements visually identifiable.
- Keep the project archive separate from the main narrative.
- Provide a useful empty or fallback state for unavailable demos and images.

### Responsive design

- Design mobile layouts intentionally; do not simply shrink the desktop layout.
- Stack multi-column content cleanly on smaller screens.
- Ensure project screenshots remain understandable on mobile.
- Keep body text and tap targets comfortable to use.
- Avoid horizontal scrolling except for a deliberate, clearly usable component.
- Test at narrow phone, standard phone, tablet, and desktop widths.

### Accessibility

- Use semantic HTML landmarks and a logical heading hierarchy.
- Ensure keyboard navigation works throughout the site.
- Provide visible keyboard focus states.
- Add meaningful alt text to informative images; use empty alt text for purely decorative images.
- Maintain sufficient text and UI contrast.
- Do not communicate status through colour alone.
- Ensure menus, dialogs, galleries, and other interactive components have accessible names and behaviour.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.

## 9. Technical implementation guidance

The portfolio should be fast and maintainable. Avoid introducing infrastructure that the site does not need.

### Suggested stack

| Concern | Suggested approach |
|---|---|
| Framework | Next.js |
| Styling | Tailwind CSS or CSS Modules; choose one primary approach |
| Animation | Motion for React where needed |
| Advanced animation | GSAP only for interactions that justify the extra complexity |
| Icons | Lucide, used consistently |
| Content | Structured TypeScript/JSON/MDX data for milestones and projects |
| Images | Optimized local assets or a suitable image service |
| Hosting | Existing Vercel deployment, if it still fits the project |

These are recommendations, not mandatory dependencies. Prefer native platform capabilities and a small dependency set where possible.

### Suggested content model

Keep timeline entries and projects in structured data rather than hardcoding every repeated layout.

A milestone could contain:

- `id`
- `date` or `period`
- `chapter`
- `title`
- `summary`
- `story`
- `lessons`
- `image`
- `relatedProjects`

A project could contain:

- `slug`
- `title`
- `summary`
- `type`
- `status`
- `role`
- `period`
- `coverImage`
- `gallery`
- `problem`
- `approach`
- `implementation`
- `technologies`
- `outcome`
- `lessons`
- `demoUrl`
- `repositoryUrl`

Use only fields that are useful. Do not add a CMS or database solely because the content can be structured.

### Performance and SEO

- Use optimized image formats and responsive image sizes.
- Lazy-load non-critical images below the fold.
- Avoid shipping large animation libraries to pages that do not need them.
- Keep client-side JavaScript limited to genuinely interactive features.
- Set a meaningful page title and meta description.
- Add Open Graph metadata for link previews.
- Use descriptive URLs for project case studies.
- Add a favicon and appropriate social preview image.
- Check broken links and missing images before release.
- Monitor Core Web Vitals and real-device performance rather than relying only on a desktop development machine.

## 10. Do and don't summary

| DO ✅ | DON'T ❌ |
|---|---|
| Tell a truthful, specific story. | Invent milestones or achievements. |
| Connect skills to real evidence. | Use arbitrary skill percentages. |
| Feature 3–5 strong projects. | Put every small experiment in the hero or main project area. |
| Explain the problem and technical decisions. | Show only screenshots and technology badges. |
| Use real screenshots and photographs. | Depend on generic stock imagery to represent personal work. |
| Keep navigation and contact obvious. | Force recruiters to read the whole story to find projects. |
| Use restrained motion. | Animate everything or hijack scrolling. |
| Design mobile and accessibility intentionally. | Treat mobile as a compressed desktop layout. |
| Optimise images and JavaScript. | Add dependencies and effects without a clear purpose. |
| Keep content current and accurate. | Leave outdated dates, goals, or status descriptions online. |
| Explain my actual contribution. | Claim team work as individual work. |
| Use a consistent visual system. | Mix unrelated styles, fonts, colours, and component patterns. |

## 12. Recommended workflow

Build the portfolio in stages. Do not start by polishing animations before the content and structure are clear.

### Phase 1 — Collect the story

- [ ] Write down the important stages of my journey in chronological order.
- [ ] Confirm dates and facts where possible.
- [ ] Identify turning points, challenges, and meaningful discoveries.
- [ ] Collect photographs, screenshots, notes, and other authentic material.
- [ ] Remove details I do not want to publish.

### Phase 2 — Audit projects

- [ ] List every project worth considering.
- [ ] Record the purpose, my role, technologies, status, and links for each.
- [ ] Select 3–5 projects for the homepage.
- [ ] Identify which projects deserve a full case study.
- [ ] Capture or create accurate screenshots and diagrams.
- [ ] Check that public repositories and demos do not expose secrets or private data.

### Phase 3 — Define the design system

- [ ] Choose the final colour palette.
- [ ] Choose heading, body, and monospace fonts.
- [ ] Define spacing, grid, container widths, borders, and radii.
- [ ] Define buttons, links, navigation, badges, project cards, and chapter layouts.
- [ ] Check contrast and responsive behaviour before building every section.

### Phase 4 — Build the structure

- [ ] Implement navigation and page sections.
- [ ] Build reusable milestone and project components.
- [ ] Add the real story and project content.
- [ ] Create individual project case-study pages where appropriate.
- [ ] Add the CV, contact details, and profile links.

### Phase 5 — Add interactions and polish

- [ ] Add only the interactions that improve understanding or navigation.
- [ ] Add reduced-motion support.
- [ ] Optimise images and loading behaviour.
- [ ] Refine typography, spacing, and mobile layouts.

### Phase 6 — Test and launch

- [ ] Test on desktop, tablet, and mobile.
- [ ] Test keyboard navigation and visible focus.
- [ ] Check image alt text and colour contrast.
- [ ] Check all internal and external links.
- [ ] Verify project descriptions and claims for accuracy.
- [ ] Check page titles, descriptions, and social previews.
- [ ] Test performance on a real mobile device.
- [ ] Proofread the entire site.
- [ ] Remove placeholder content before publishing.

## 13. Final quality checklist

Before calling the redesign complete, answer **yes** to these questions:

### Story
- [ ] Does the opening explain who I am and what the site is about?
- [ ] Does the journey reflect real experiences rather than generic career statements?
- [ ] Does every chapter contribute something meaningful?
- [ ] Can a visitor understand how I developed as a person and developer?

### Projects and skills
- [ ] Are the strongest projects easy to find?
- [ ] Does each featured project explain the problem, my contribution, and the result?
- [ ] Are technical claims supported by evidence?
- [ ] Are unfinished projects clearly labelled?

### Design and UX
- [ ] Is the visual identity consistent and recognisable?
- [ ] Is the design readable and uncluttered?
- [ ] Can visitors navigate directly to the information they need?
- [ ] Does the experience work well on mobile?
- [ ] Can the site be used without a mouse or animation?

### Engineering quality
- [ ] Are images optimised?
- [ ] Is unnecessary JavaScript avoided?
- [ ] Are metadata and social previews configured?
- [ ] Are there no broken links, placeholder sections, or console errors?
- [ ] Is the content easy to update without rewriting repeated UI?

---

## The guiding principle

**The story explains the journey. The projects prove the skills. The design makes both memorable.**

Whenever a design or content decision is unclear, prioritise clarity, authenticity, evidence, accessibility, and performance over visual effects.
